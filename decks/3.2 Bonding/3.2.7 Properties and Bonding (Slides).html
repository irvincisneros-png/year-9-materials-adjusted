Bonding Shapes Everything We Use

From salt shakers to copper wires

Bonding and structure strongly influence conductivity, melting and brittleness. This helps engineers select materials for each job.

BONDING · IONIC

What is an Ionic Substance?

Ionic: metal + non-metal, e.g. table salt (NaCl)
Positive and negative ions lock into a rigid crystal lattice
Strong attractions → high melting point and hard
BONDING · IONIC

Ionic: Key Properties

Hard but brittle — lattice shatters when layers shift
High melting point — needs lots of energy to break lattice
Conducts electricity only when dissolved or melted
BONDING · COVALENT

Covalent Substances

Non-metals share electrons — e.g. water, sugar, wax
Weak forces between molecules → low melting points
No free ions or electrons → cannot conduct electricity
BONDING · METALLIC

Metals: Key Properties

Metal ions in a sea of delocalised (free) electrons
Malleable and ductile — layers slide without breaking
Free electrons carry charge → conducts electricity + heat

Ionic vs Covalent vs Metal

Ionic
Hard, brittle crystal
High melting point
Conducts when dissolved
Metal
Malleable and ductile
High melting point
Always conducts electricity

BONDING · APPLICATIONS

Bonding Meets Real Life

Copper → electrical wiring (conducts + bends easily)
Salt → food preservation by lowering water activity
Wax → candles (covalent; melts at low temperature)

3 Things to Remember

Ionic

Hard, brittle, high melting point — conducts when dissolved

Covalent

Molecular substances often melt at lower temperatures; giant covalent structures are exceptions.

Metal

Usually malleable and conductive because of delocalised electrons; conductivity varies.
